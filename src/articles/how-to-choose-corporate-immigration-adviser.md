**Choosing the right corporate immigration adviser is one of the highest-leverage decisions a growing employer can make: the right firm keeps your workforce pipeline flowing and your compliance record clean, while the wrong one can trigger licence suspensions, civil penalties, and the loss of international candidates you spent months recruiting.** **This guide explains exactly what to look for, what to ask, what to pay, and — critically — what mistakes to avoid, whether you are hiring across borders for the first time or overhauling a programme that has grown beyond your current adviser's capabilities.**

---

## Why This Matters More in 2026

The landscape for employers sponsoring international workers has become materially more complex over the past two years. In the UK, the Home Office tightened compliance expectations following a wave of licence revocations in 2024–2025, and civil penalty thresholds for illegal working were raised significantly. In the United States, USCIS processing unpredictability, continued H-1B oversubscription, and shifting enforcement priorities have made petition strategy — not just paperwork completion — the difference between securing a key hire and losing them to a competitor.

At the same time, many employers are managing multi-jurisdiction programmes simultaneously. A tech company might be sponsoring software engineers on UK Skilled Worker visas, filing H-1B petitions in the US, and exploring [Germany work visa routes](/germany-work-visa-routes-employers/) for a new European R&D hub — all at once. Coordinating this across three legal systems with different timelines, fee structures, and compliance obligations requires a level of expertise that no generalist HR department can reasonably be expected to hold.

The good news: the corporate immigration advisory market has matured. There are excellent firms at every price point. The challenge is knowing how to evaluate them.

## Step One: Clarify What You Actually Need

Before you speak to a single adviser, write a one-page internal brief that answers these questions:

- **Which countries** will you be sponsoring workers into?
- **What visa routes** are most relevant (e.g., UK Skilled Worker, US H-1B or O-1, EU Blue Card)?
- **What volume** of cases do you anticipate in the next 12 months?
- **What is your internal HR capacity?** Do you have someone who can own the relationship with the adviser day-to-day?
- **What is your compliance maturity?** Are you a first-time sponsor applicant, or an established sponsor with existing obligations?

This brief does two things. First, it helps you approach the right category of firm. A boutique UK immigration solicitor is ideal for a 20-person business filing four Certificates of Sponsorship a year; they are probably not the right fit for a multinational coordinating 300 sponsored workers across six jurisdictions. Second, it gives you a shared reference point during adviser pitches, so you are comparing like for like.

## Understanding the Regulatory Landscape: UK vs. US

The regulatory frameworks governing who can legally advise employers differ significantly between jurisdictions, and understanding this protects you from unqualified providers.

### United Kingdom

In the UK, anyone providing immigration advice or services for a fee must be either:

- A **solicitor** regulated by the Solicitors Regulation Authority (SRA), or
- An **OISC-registered adviser** at the appropriate level (Level 3 for complex casework including sponsor licensing)

Operating outside this framework is a criminal offence. You can verify SRA registration at sra.org.uk and OISC registration at gov.uk/find-an-immigration-adviser. Always check before you engage.

Corporate immigration work in the UK typically centres on sponsor licence applications, [Certificates of Sponsorship](/certificate-of-sponsorship-explained-employers/), right-to-work compliance, and Home Office audit preparation.

### United States

In the US, only a **licensed attorney** (or an accredited representative working for a recognised non-profit) may charge a fee for immigration legal advice. There is no equivalent of the OISC — the distinction is straightforwardly attorney vs. non-attorney. Some HR technology platforms offer "immigration support" that stops short of legal advice; understand clearly where their service ends and where you need a licensed attorney to take over.

For employers, US corporate immigration work typically involves I-129 petitions, PERM labour certification, I-140 immigrant petitions, and I-9 compliance audits.

Not all firms are structured the same way. Here is a practical comparison of the main categories you will encounter:

| Adviser Type | Best For | Typical Strengths | Watch Out For |

| **Large international law firm** | Multinationals, complex multi-country programmes | Global reach, specialist teams per jurisdiction, litigation capacity | High fees, junior associates on day-to-day work, less responsive for small volumes |
| **Mid-size specialist immigration firm** | Growth-stage businesses, 10–100 sponsored workers/year | Deep route expertise, senior-led cases, competitive pricing | May lack capacity for very high volumes or niche jurisdictions |

| **OISC-registered consultancy (non-solicitor)** | Routine UK cases, volume processing at lower cost | Lower cost per case for standard routes | Cannot handle litigation or complex appeals; ensure Level 3 registration for sponsor work |

## The Seven Criteria That Actually Matter

### 1. Verified Regulatory Standing

This is non-negotiable. Ask for the firm's SRA number, OISC registration number, or state bar number before any other conversation. Verify it independently. A firm that cannot produce this immediately is disqualified.

### 2. Demonstrable Expertise in Your Specific Routes

"Immigration" is a broad field. An adviser who is excellent on UK spouse visas may have limited experience with sponsor licence compliance. Ask specifically: *How many sponsor licence applications have you submitted in the last 12 months? What is your first-time approval rate? Have you handled compliance visits?*

For US employers, ask how many H-1B petitions they filed in the most recent fiscal year, and whether they have PERM experience if you are planning green card sponsorship.

### 3. Named Caseworker Accountability

One of the most common complaints employers have about immigration firms is that they are sold to by a senior partner and then handed off to a junior associate. Ask directly: *Who will be the named caseworker on my account? What is their experience level? What happens if they leave the firm?*

### 4. Proactive Policy Monitoring

Immigration policy changes frequently and sometimes without warning. Your adviser should be sending you proactive updates when changes affect your programme — not waiting for you to ask. Ask how they communicate policy changes and request examples of recent client advisories.

### 5. Technology and Case Management

In 2026, a well-run immigration firm uses case management software that gives you real-time visibility into application status, upcoming deadlines, and document expiry dates. If a firm is managing your cases via email threads and spreadsheets alone, that is a risk indicator for a high-volume programme.

### 6. References From Comparable Employers

Ask for two or three references from businesses of similar size, sector, and hiring pattern to yours. Speak to those references directly. Ask: *Did the adviser flag problems before they became crises? Did they meet deadlines? How did they handle mistakes?*

### 7. Fee Transparency

A professional firm will give you a clear written fee schedule covering all service types you are likely to need. Be wary of firms that give only verbal estimates, bundle everything into an opaque retainer, or are vague about disbursements (government filing fees, courier costs, etc.). ## Illustrative Example: Choosing an Adviser for a 50-Person Tech Scale-Up

*The following is an illustrative scenario. Figures are for illustration only and do not represent guarantees or predictions.*

**The situation:** A software company based in London with 50 employees wants to hire six international engineers in the next year — four from outside the UK and two from the EU. They have no existing sponsor licence.

**Option A — Large International Law Firm:** Quoted £6,500 for the sponsor licence application plus £1,200 per Certificate of Sponsorship. Senior partner leads the pitch; a newly qualified associate would handle day-to-day work. Minimum annual retainer required.

**Option B — Mid-Size Specialist Immigration Firm:** Quoted £2,800 for the sponsor licence application plus £850 per Certificate of Sponsorship. Named senior caseworker with five years of sponsor licence experience. No retainer required; pay per service. Provides a client portal with real-time case tracking.

**Option C — OISC Level 2 Consultancy:** Quoted £1,400 for the sponsor licence application. Adviser holds Level 2 OISC registration only — insufficient for sponsor licence casework at full complexity. *This option is immediately disqualified on regulatory grounds.*

**Analysis:** For this employer's needs and volume, Option B represents strong value. Option A may become appropriate if the company scales to 30+ sponsored workers annually and needs litigation capacity. Option C is ineligible regardless of price.

**Illustrative first-year cost under Option B:** £2,800 (licence) + (6 × £850 CoS) = £7,900 in adviser fees, before government fees. This is a known, predictable cost — far lower than the estimated £15,000–£25,000 cost of a refused licence application and delayed hiring programme.

## Common Mistakes Employers Make — and How to Avoid Them

### 1. Choosing on Price Alone

**The mistake:** Selecting the cheapest quote without checking regulatory credentials or caseworker experience.

**The solution:** Use price as a data point, not a decision driver. Once you have verified credentials and experience, compare value — what you get for the fee, not just the fee itself.

### 2. Failing to Check the Regulatory Register

**The mistake:** Taking the adviser's word that they are regulated, without independent verification.

**The solution:** Spend five minutes on the SRA, OISC, or state bar website before any further conversation. No exceptions.

### 3. Not Asking Who Will Actually Work on Your Cases

**The mistake:** Being impressed by a senior partner in the pitch meeting, then finding your cases are handled by someone with six months' experience.

**The solution:** Ask in writing: *Who is the named caseworker, what is their experience, and what is the escalation path if I am unsatisfied?*

### 4. Underestimating the Ongoing Compliance Obligation

**The mistake:** Treating immigration advice as a one-off purchase — hire an adviser to get the licence, then manage everything in-house.

**The solution:** Understand that sponsor licence compliance is continuous. Sponsors have reporting duties, right-to-work check obligations, and record-keeping requirements that persist indefinitely. An adviser who supports you through the application but disappears afterwards leaves you exposed. Read more about [what a sponsor licence lets you do once approved](/what-sponsor-licence-lets-you-do-once-approved/) and the [ongoing compliance duties that follow approval](/sponsor-licence-compliance-duties-after-approval/).

### 5. Not Briefing the Adviser on Your Business Model

**The mistake:** Providing minimal information and expecting the adviser to produce a strong application or petition.

**The solution:** Give your adviser a thorough business briefing: your corporate structure, HR policies, job descriptions, salary bandings, and hiring plans. The more context they have, the stronger your applications will be.

### 6. Ignoring the Onboarding and Integration Dimension

**The mistake:** Treating immigration as isolated from the rest of your international hiring process.

**The solution:** Your adviser should coordinate with your HR team, not operate in a silo. Immigration timelines must feed into your [onboarding planning for relocating employees](/how-to-onboard-employee-relocating-from-abroad/) and your overall offer management process.

### 7. Waiting Until You Need a Visa to Find an Adviser

**The mistake:** Starting the search for an adviser only after you have made a job offer to an international candidate and are under time pressure.

**The solution:** Identify and engage your adviser before you begin international recruitment. A good adviser will help you structure your [hiring process for foreign workers](/how-to-hire-foreign-workers-legally/) from the outset, including role eligibility assessment and salary compliance checks.

## The Engagement Letter: What Should Be in It

Once you have chosen an adviser, insist on a written engagement letter before paying anything. It should cover:

- **Scope of services:** Precisely which applications, filings, and advisory services are included
- **Named caseworkers:** Who is responsible for your account
- **Fee schedule:** All fees, disbursements, and circumstances that would trigger additional charges
- **Timeline commitments:** Expected turnaround times for each service type
- **Communication protocol:** How and how quickly they will respond to queries
- **Data protection:** How they will handle employee personal data (critical for GDPR compliance in the UK/EU)
- **Termination clause:** How you exit the relationship and transfer files if needed

A firm that resists providing a clear engagement letter is a firm to avoid.

## Multi-Jurisdiction Programmes: Special Considerations

If your business is sponsoring workers in more than one country simultaneously, your adviser selection becomes more complex. A few principles apply:

**Coordination matters more than consolidation.** It is tempting to give everything to one large firm for simplicity, but the best-in-class specialist for US H-1B work may not be the strongest choice for UK Skilled Worker visas. For many employers, a coordinating arrangement — one primary contact who manages referrals to specialist local counsel — delivers better outcomes than a single firm trying to do everything.

**Understand who holds the licence in each jurisdiction.** Global mobility consultancies often subcontract the actual legal work. Know the name of the licensed attorney or OISC-regulated solicitor handling each matter, not just the consultancy.

**Build in lead times.** Different visa routes have dramatically different processing times. [How long a sponsor licence application takes](/how-long-does-sponsor-licence-application-take/) in the UK differs from USCIS processing for an H-1B petition. Your adviser should give you a consolidated timeline view so you can make hiring decisions with accurate expectations.

## Red Flags: Walk Away If You See These

- **Guaranteed approval:** No legitimate adviser will guarantee a visa approval. Outcomes are determined by the relevant government authority, not the adviser.
- **No regulatory number:** Non-negotiable. Walk away.
- **Pressure to sign quickly:** Reputable firms do not use high-pressure sales tactics.
- **Vague about who will handle your cases:** If they cannot name a caseworker before you sign, they cannot be held accountable after.
- **No written engagement letter:** Verbal agreements in legal matters are always a risk.
- **No questions about your business:** An adviser who quotes without asking about your structure, roles, and HR processes is not doing a proper assessment.
- **Unable to explain the compliance obligations:** If they cannot fluently explain [right-to-work check requirements](/right-to-work-checks-sponsored-employees/) or what triggers a reporting duty, they lack the depth you need.

## Building an Internal Capability Alongside External Advice

The best employer-adviser relationships are partnerships, not dependencies. As your international hiring scales, you should be building internal knowledge alongside external expertise. Designate an internal HR owner for immigration matters. Ensure they understand the basics of your visa routes, your compliance calendar, and how to use your adviser's case management portal.

This serves two purposes. First, it makes your programme more resilient — if your adviser relationship breaks down, you are not starting from zero. Second, it reduces your adviser's time (and your cost) on basic queries, freeing them to focus on complex strategy.

Resources like our guides on [sponsoring an employee for a work visa step by step](/sponsoring-employee-work-visa-step-by-step/) and [how to hire foreign workers legally in the US](/how-to-hire-foreign-workers-legally/) are useful internal training tools for HR teams building this knowledge base.

## Reviewing and Switching Advisers

Even a strong relationship needs periodic review. Conduct a formal assessment at least annually. Key questions:

- Have they proactively flagged policy changes that affect us?
- Have applications been filed accurately and on time?
- Are fees in line with the original engagement letter?
- Is our caseworker still at the firm, and are we satisfied with their successor if not?
- Is the firm's technology and process keeping pace with our programme's complexity?

Switching advisers mid-programme is manageable with proper file transfer. Ensure your engagement letter includes a clause requiring the outgoing firm to transfer all files promptly upon termination. Your new adviser will need complete application histories, correspondence with government authorities, and copies of all submitted documents.

## Summary: A Quick Vetting Checklist

Before signing with any corporate immigration adviser, confirm the following:

- [ ] Regulatory registration verified independently (SRA / OISC / state bar)
- [ ] Specific experience confirmed in your relevant visa routes
- [ ] Named caseworker identified with verifiable experience
- [ ] Client references provided and contacted
- [ ] Written fee schedule received covering all anticipated services
- [ ] Engagement letter reviewed and signed
- [ ] Case management technology demonstrated
- [ ] Proactive policy update process explained
- [ ] Clear escalation and complaints procedure confirmed
- [ ] Data protection / GDPR compliance confirmed in writing

The investment in rigorous vetting at the outset pays for itself many times over in avoided errors, smoother applications, and a compliance record that protects your ability to keep sponsoring workers for years to come.
